## Risk Analysis

### 🔴 Critical

**1. Restrictive Covenant Agreement — Forfeiture + Disgorgement (Exhibit B, §§1–5)**

> "If, during the Restricted Period, the Grantee discloses or uses… any Confidential Information other than in the course of performing authorized services… the Performance Units… whether vested or not, shall be immediately forfeited and cancelled, and the Grantee shall immediately return to the Company the Common Shares received in connection with the settlement… or the pre-tax income derived from any disposition of the Common Shares or the pre-tax cash amount…"

- **Issue:** The "Restricted Period" runs from the **start of the Performance Period** (5/1/20__) through **one year after settlement** — potentially a **~4-year tail**. Breach triggers: (a) forfeiture of all unvested units, (b) forfeiture of vested-but-unsettled units, and (c) **disgorgement of the *pre-tax* (i.e., gross) proceeds** of any shares already sold.
- **Risk:** A single act of inadvertent disclosure or service to a "Conflicting Organization" (defined expansively) could claw back the **entire economic value** of the PSU grant — including shares sold years earlier. The non-compete (Section 5) has **no geographic limitation** and includes a rebuttable presumption that the Grantee had knowledge of any Confidential Information to which they were "directly exposed."
- **Market Standard:** Restrictive covenants enforced by *forfeiture* of unvested equity are common in C-suite PSU agreements. **Disgorgement of *gross* proceeds of shares already sold and a 4-year reach-back is on the aggressive end.** Most market agreements (a) measure the Restricted Period from separation, not grant, (b) cap disgorgement at net-of-tax proceeds, and (c) require the Company to demonstrate actual competitive harm.
- **Negotiability:** Medium — Smucker will defend these provisions, but the period and disgorgement scope are negotiable in executive packages.
- **Redline suggestions:**
  - "Restricted Period" should run from **termination of Continuous Service** through **12 months thereafter** (not from Performance Period start).
  - "Return" should be capped at **net-after-tax proceeds** of the specific PSU shares (not all current shareholdings).
  - Non-compete should add a **geographic limitation** (states/countries where Grantee worked or Company does business) and a **general-advertising / passive-investment carve-out** beyond the existing media carve-out.

**2. Discretionary Grant Acknowledgment & Claim Waiver (Article III, §15)**

> "in consideration of the grant of the Performance Units, no claim or entitlement to compensation or damages shall be created by any forfeiture or other termination of the Performance Units or diminution in value of the Performance Units… If any such claim is found by a court of competent jurisdiction to have been created, then, by signing this Agreement, the Grantee shall be deemed irrevocably to have waived the Grantee's entitlement to pursue such claim."

- **Issue:** A sweeping, irrevocable waiver of claims for any adverse action related to the award, including **diminution in value** caused by Company action.
- **Risk:** Could bar wrongful-termination, breach-of-contract, or Section 409A penalty claims tied to the award. The "irrevocable waiver" language is unusually strong.
- **Market Standard:** Discretionary-grant acknowledgments are standard. **An irrevocable, pre-emptive waiver of all related claims (including for Company-caused diminution) is more aggressive than typical.** Most agreements limit the waiver to claims *arising from the grant itself*, not from *underlying employment actions that happen to affect the award's value*.
- **Negotiability:** Low–Medium.
- **Redline:** Limit the waiver to claims "arising solely from the discretionary nature of the grant" and exclude claims for breach of contract, securities-law violations, or wrongful termination *as those claims affect the award*.

**3. Unilateral Plan Amendment (Article III, §12)**

> "Any amendment to the Plan shall be deemed to be an amendment to this Agreement to the extent that the amendment is applicable hereto; provided, however, that no amendment shall impair the rights of the Grantee under this Agreement without the Grantee's consent; further provided, however, that the Grantee's consent shall not be required to an amendment that is deemed necessary by the Company to ensure compliance with (or exemption from) Section 409A… or the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act…"

- **Issue:** Plan amendments flow through automatically. The "impair" standard is undefined and likely Company-friendly. 409A and Dodd-Frank are open-ended justifications for non-consensual change.
- **Risk:** The Committee could, in theory, change the performance metrics, vesting schedule, settlement form, or restrictive covenants by amending the Plan — as long as they frame the change as a 409A or Dodd-Frank "compliance" matter.
- **Market Standard:** Most public-company PSU agreements require Grantee consent for amendments that **materially and adversely affect** vested or accruing rights.
- **Negotiability:** Low.
- **Redline:** Tighten "impair" to "materially and adversely affect," and require that any 409A/Dodd-Frank amendment be the minimum necessary to achieve compliance.

**4. Forfeiture for "Detrimental Activity" (Article II, §3(a))**

> "The Performance Units (and corresponding Dividend Equivalents) may also be forfeited in the event the Committee determines the Grantee has engaged in Detrimental Activity as such term is defined in the Plan."

- **Issue:** "Detrimental Activity" is **not defined in this agreement** — only in the Plan, which is not attached. Committee makes the determination with no objective standard set out here.
- **Risk:** Could be construed very broadly (competitive activity, disparagement, disclosure of confidential information, etc.), giving the Committee a wide post-hoc forfeiture power.
- **Market Standard:** Public-company PSU agreements typically either (a) include the Detrimental Activity definition inline, or (b) limit it to enumerated categories (willful misconduct, felony, material breach of confidentiality).
- **Negotiability:** Low–Medium.
- **Redline:** Require the Plan to be attached; or insert a narrower inline definition (e.g., "willful misconduct involving the Company, conviction of a felony, or material breach of the Restrictive Covenant Agreement").

### 🟡 Important

**5. Change in Control Vesting at Target Only (Article II, §3(d))**

> "if a Change in Control occurs, then the Grantee shall vest in all of the Performance Units at the Target Level with such vesting to occur upon the consummation of the Change in Control."

- **Issue:** CIC acceleration is **at Target only**, ignoring actual or projected performance. In a strong-performing company, this can mean giving up significant upside.
- **Market Standard:** Many large-cap public companies provide **the greater of Target and actual projected performance** at CIC. Some also offer double-trigger acceleration (CIC + qualifying termination within a window).
- **Negotiability:** Medium.
- **Redline:** "vest in the greater of (i) the Target Units and (ii) the Vesting Eligible Units determined by the Committee based on performance through the date of the Change in Control."

**6. Settlement Form at Committee's Sole Discretion (Article II, §4(a))**

- **Issue:** Cash vs. shares is at the Committee's discretion, not the Grantee's. This creates **tax-timing uncertainty** (cash triggers ordinary income at receipt; shares may allow deferral strategies).
- **Market Standard:** Many agreements let the Grantee elect, or at least default to shares.
- **Negotiability:** Low.
- **Redline:** "…shall, **at the Grantee's election** (subject to the Company's compliance with Section 409A), issue Common Shares…"

**7. No "Good Reason" Termination Concept (Article II, §3)**

- **Issue:** The agreement recognizes a "Termination Event" defined as termination *for the convenience of the Company*. It does not address termination for *good reason* (e.g., material diminution in role, compensation, or relocation). The Grantee cannot claim accelerated or pro-rata vesting if they resign for cause attributable to the Company.
- **Market Standard:** Executive PSU agreements typically include good-reason treatment, often paired with CIC ("double trigger").
- **Negotiability:** Medium.
- **Redline:** Add a definition of "Good Reason" and provide for pro-rata vesting on par with §3(b) or (e).

**8. Clawback of Incentive Compensation (Article III, §15(f))**

- **Issue:** Covered Employees are subject to the **Clawback of Incentive Compensation Policy**, which is not attached.
- **Risk:** Without the Policy text, you cannot evaluate whether clawback is limited to restatement-driven overpayment or extends to misconduct, reputational harm, or even operational decisions.
- **Market Standard:** Post-Dodd-Frank, most clawback policies are limited to financial-restatement scenarios. Some are broader.
- **Negotiability:** Low.
- **Redline:** Require the Policy to be attached; limit clawback to situations involving a financial restatement caused by Grantee misconduct.

**9. Continuous Service & Leave of Absence (Article III, §9)**

- **Issue:** A leave of absence is "approved by a duly constituted officer of the Company or a Subsidiary" — sole discretion.
- **Risk:** Unapproved leaves (medical, family) could trigger a Continuous Service break and forfeiture.
- **Market Standard:** Modern agreements typically provide for protected leaves under FMLA, military, or other legally required categories.
- **Negotiability:** Low.
- **Redline:** Add "or as required by applicable law (including FMLA, USERRA, and state/foreign leave entitlements)."

**10. Settlement Window (Article II, §4(a))**

- **Issue:** Settlement can occur up to **March 5 of the year following the end of the Performance Period** — potentially ~10 months after the Determination Date.
- **Risk:** Adverse tax consequences if the Company delays; the 10-day "as soon as practicable" language gives the Company wide scheduling flexibility.
- **Negotiability:** Low.
- **Redline:** Cap delay at 30 (not "10 days after… March 5").

- **Why it matters:** "Detrimental Activity," "Change in Control," "Market Value per Share," "Subsidiary," the Clawback Policy, and Section 16 transfer restrictions all live in the Plan. **You cannot evaluate your actual exposure without it.**

**2. Dispute Resolution / Claim Adjudication Mechanism**

- **Why it matters:** No mediation, arbitration, or even internal-review step before litigation. Disputes default to Ohio courts.
- **What could go wrong:** The Grantee must litigate every dispute (e.g., a Detrimental Activity determination or a Clawback claim) in Ohio, which is expensive and may not be convenient.
- **Suggested language (new Section 26):**
  > "**Dispute Resolution.** Before initiating any action arising out of or relating to this Agreement, the parties shall first attempt in good faith to resolve the dispute through non-binding mediation administered by JAMS under its Employment Mediation Rules, held virtually or at a mutually agreed location. **3. Excise Tax (Section 280G) Treatment**

- **Why it matters:** CIC vesting can trigger 280G "golden parachute" excise taxes (typically 20% on excess parachute payments).
- **What could go wrong:** Senior executives can lose a substantial portion of CIC value to excise taxes.
- **Suggested language (new Section 27):**
  > "**280G Treatment.** If the Grantee would be subject to the excise tax under Section 4999 of the Code in connection with a Change in Control, the Company shall (a) pay the Grantee an additional amount (a 'Gross-Up Payment') sufficient such that the net after-tax amount retained by the Grantee, after payment of all federal, state, local and excise taxes on the Gross-Up Payment, equals the after-tax value of the payments and benefits the Grantee would have received absent the excise tax; or, if greater, (b) reduce the payments and benefits such that no portion thereof is subject to the excise tax, whichever yields the Grantee a greater after-tax amount. The calculation shall be performed by the Company's independent auditors."
